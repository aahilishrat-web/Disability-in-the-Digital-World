Disability in the Digital World

The perception and representation of disability on social media

Does social media reinforce ableist assumptions about disability, or does it challenge them?

Introduction

Plain language summarySocial media shapes how people think about disability. Some posts let disabled people tell their own stories. Other posts use stereotypes or make jokes about disabled people. This page looks at both.

Social media has become one of the most influential ways people learn about and understand the world around them. Platforms such as TikTok, Instagram, and YouTube let individuals share experiences, educate others, and challenge perspectives. For people with disabilities, these platforms create chances to speak about their lives, push back on stereotypes, and show different perspectives on disability. Creators share their experiences with assistive technology, talk about daily life, and build communities.

Not all disability representation on social media is positive. Some content still relies on stereotypes, portrays disabled people as helpless, or treats everyday activities as inspirational simply because a disabled person is doing them. These portrayals affect how audiences understand disability and can feed ableist assumptions.

What this page covers

We look at both the positive and negative sides of disability representation on social media, especially how social media can:

  • Challenge stereotypes and change how people understand disability online
  • Promote disability pride, creativity, and self-expression
  • Show how assistive technology can support access and independence
  • Reinforce ableist ideas through harmful stereotypes or narrow representation

We use Disability Studies, the critical lens for studying disability, and Disability Justice, a framework for collective liberation. We also use the medical, social, and affirmative models of disability to explain how disability is represented online and why those representations matter.

Key terms

Plain language summaryThese are the ideas we use to study each post. The medical model says the problem is in the body. The social model says the problem is the barriers around the person. The affirmative model says disability can be a positive part of who someone is.

Medical model of disability
Treats disability as a problem inside a person's body or mind that a professional should diagnose, treat, or cure. Success means getting closer to "normal."
Murza (2025) contrasts this model with the social model, especially for neurodivergent people.
Social model of disability
Separates impairment (a difference in the body or mind) from disability (the barriers a society builds around that difference). Stairs without a ramp disable a wheelchair user. Fixing the environment is the goal.
Murza (2025); Parey et al. (2025) apply the social model alongside an ethics of care framework to families of people with lower-limb amputations.
Affirmative model of disability
Goes a step further than the social model and treats disability as a valid, sometimes positive, part of identity and community, rather than a tragedy.
Swain & French (2000).
Inspiration porn
Content that presents disabled people as inspiring simply for existing or doing ordinary things, mainly so nondisabled viewers can feel moved or feel better about their own lives.
The term was popularized by disabled journalist and comedian Stella Young (2014).
Infantilization
Treating a disabled adult like a child: speaking for them, doing tasks for them without asking, or assuming they cannot know their own abilities.
Invisible disability
A disability that is not obvious to an onlooker, such as chronic pain, fatigue, heart conditions, or many neurological conditions. People with invisible disabilities are often accused of faking.
Ableism
Beliefs and practices that treat nondisabled bodies and minds as the standard and disabled people as lesser.

Part 1: Speaking for ourselves

Plain language summaryThese four examples show disabled people, or the technology they use, in a positive way. Most of them are made by disabled people themselves. We also ask what each example leaves out.

The posts in this section are mostly created by or centered on disabled people talking about their own lives. They push back against tragedy and cure narratives. We still look at each one critically, since positive content can carry its own assumptions.

Case study 1 · Student journalism Challenges ableism

"My autism diagnosis is not a problem to be solved"

Analysis

The title alone rejects the medical model. A diagnosis is usually framed as the first step toward treatment or a cure. This essay frames the diagnosis as information about who the author is, which matches the affirmative model's view of disability as part of human diversity. Murza (2025) makes a similar case for neurodiversity-affirming practice, which plays to autistic people's strengths instead of trying to make them look less autistic.

The essay is also written by an autistic person, not about one. In a media landscape where autism is often explained by parents, doctors, or charities, the author speaking for themself is itself a form of representation.

The author's own accessibility plan for the essay includes screen-reader compatibility, alt text for photographs, adjustable font sizes, text-to-speech, high-contrast text, a plain-language summary, and a distraction-free reading mode for readers with cognitive disabilities or sensory sensitivities. This webpage borrows several of those ideas.

Case study 2 · TikTok Challenges, with a question

A mechanical prosthetic hand by Ian Davis

Analysis

The prosthetic hand shows how assistive technology can support access, independence, and bodily autonomy for people with limb differences. The design keeps the wearer's own thumb and builds the device around the hand that exists, adapting technology to one person's body instead of forcing the body to fit a standard part.

From a Disability Studies view, the video also points to creativity and to disabled people as active participants in designing their own technology, not just patients who receive it.

A question to ask: The video is about the device. We see a hand, but we never hear from the person wearing it. Content like this can slide into a "technology will fix disability" story, which is a high-tech version of the cure narrative. The most useful prosthetics content pairs the engineering with the wearer's own account of what the device does and does not do for them.

Case study 3 · TikTok Challenges ableism

"Making my Spider-Man prosthetic leg"

Analysis

This video treats a prosthetic as something to show off, not hide. Medical design has long tried to make prosthetics look like skin so the disability "disappears." A Spider-Man leg does the opposite. It is personal, fun, and visible.

Under the affirmative model, the design celebrates disability identity and challenges negative stereotypes about prosthetic devices. It also shows bodily autonomy: the creator controls how their assistive technology looks. The "Build a Leg" playlist adds an educational layer, letting viewers see the process instead of only the finished product.

The creator is not framed as tragic or as inspirational. They are a Spider-Man fan who happens to wear a prosthetic leg, which is the kind of everyday, interest-driven representation our closing section argues for.

Case study 4 · TikTok Challenges ableism

A quad amputee veteran's top 5 life-changing prosthetics

Analysis

The video positions Mills as an expert. He is not being explained by a doctor or narrator. He is ranking and reviewing the technology he uses every day, based on his own lived experience and preferences. Under the affirmative model, he is defined by his knowledge, not only by his disability.

The social model fits too. Each device he ranks removes a barrier to a specific everyday task, which shifts attention from "what is wrong with his body" to "what tools and environments let him participate."

Prosthetic life also involves other people: fittings, repairs, family members, and caregivers. Parey et al. (2025) studied relatives of people with lower-limb amputations through an ethics of care and the social model, a reminder that this kind of independence is built through interdependence.

A question to ask: Disabled veterans are often framed as heroes who "overcame" their injuries, and that framing can turn into inspiration porn. This video mostly avoids that by staying practical. It also raises an access question: veterans can get prosthetic care through the VA, while many civilians with limb loss depend on insurance that may cover far less. Whose prosthetic stories get told on social media depends partly on who can afford the devices.

Part 2: Ableism in the feed

Plain language summaryThese three examples show how people treat disabled people unfairly, online and in daily life. One is a joke that makes fun of a disabled student. Two are disabled creators explaining what unfair treatment feels like.

Social media can spread ableism quickly, especially through jokes and memes that get shared without much thought. The same platforms also let disabled people talk back. This section includes one example of each and one that sits in between.

Case study 5 · Instagram Exposes ableism

"It's condescending and infantilizing"

Analysis

This post gives a first-hand account of how disabled people are infantilized in everyday life. The mobility aid becomes a signal to strangers that the person is helpless. Even when the person says they can do the task, their word is overruled.

The post was submitted anonymously to the Casual Ableism account, which collects these everyday experiences from disabled followers. The account also models good access practice: every image comes with a written transcription and image description in the caption.

It shows how society builds a perception of disabled people as "less than." The same mindset produces inspiration porn: if a disabled person is assumed to be incapable, then anything they do becomes "inspiring" or "moving."

The post also makes a point about help. Disability Justice values interdependence, meaning everyone relies on others. But help that ignores what the person asked for is not interdependence. It is control.

Case study 6 · TikTok Reinforces ableism

"POV: You're at the school talent show and the special ed kid performs"

Analysis

This clip is meant to be funny, and the joke depends on disabled students. It shows the prejudice people still hold. Throughout history, disabled people were used as entertainment, from court jesters for the nobility to the freak shows of the 1800s and early 1900s. Times have changed, but this kind of humor shows that the mindset has not fully gone away. It has moved to a new format.

The disabled student never appears in the video. He exists only as an imagined performance that nondisabled people react to. That is the same structure Stella Young (2014) criticized in inspiration porn: the disabled person is a prop for someone else's feelings, whether those feelings are pity, laughter, or being moved.

The phrase "special ed kid" also turns a school service into a label for a whole person, and the meme format makes the joke easy to copy and share without anyone stopping to think about who it targets. The video is tagged #comedy and #darkhumour and had over 580,000 likes when we viewed it, which shows how widely this kind of joke travels.

Case study 7 · TikTok Pushes back

"Disabled people don't always look it"

Analysis

The first half of the video repeats a common ableist assumption: disability has to be visible to be real. Many disabilities are not visible, and the public is not owed a look at anyone's medical records. Judging someone by what they can or can't do in a parking lot reflects how society decides what counts as "normal" and treats anything outside that box as suspicious.

The judging clip was originally posted by a different account, @joypatrol, whose watermark is visible in the first scene. The second half is a disabled creator answering back. TikTok's response and stitch features let her take a clip that judges a stranger and turn it into a lesson. She also defends someone she does not know and whose disability she cannot see, which is a form of cross-disability solidarity.

The video has burned-in captions, which helps Deaf and hard-of-hearing viewers. One caption is cut off at the edge of the frame, a small example of how access features need checking too.

What these posts teach audiences

Plain language summaryPosts teach people ideas about disability, even when they don't mean to. Here are the main lessons we found across all seven examples.

Every post on social media teaches its viewers something about disabled people, whether or not the creator intends it. Looking across our seven examples, these are the lessons audiences are being taught.

Technology and prosthetics

Cases 2, 3, and 4 teach that prosthetics can be tools, art, and personal style. The risk is a story where technology "fixes" disability. The posts that avoid this let the user speak about what the device does for their daily life.

Cure versus acceptance

The medical model teaches audiences to see disability as something to cure. The autism essay in case 1 rejects that and teaches that a diagnosis can be a source of self-understanding instead of a problem.

Heroism and tragedy

Disabled people are often shown as either tragic or heroic. Case 6 uses a disabled student as a source of pity or laughter, and veteran stories like case 4 can slide toward "overcoming" narratives. Both make disability about the nondisabled viewer's feelings.

Dependence and care

Case 5 shows the harm of help that ignores consent, while case 4 shows care networks that support independence. The lesson for audiences is to ask before helping and to see everyone as interdependent.

Who counts as disabled

Case 7 shows that audiences have been taught disability must be visible. The creator's response teaches the opposite and models solidarity with a stranger whose disability she cannot see.

Community and liberation

When disabled creators post for each other, as in cases 3, 5, and 7, social media becomes a place for community, shared knowledge, and pride, which points toward the collective liberation Disability Justice describes.

The Disability Justice framework

Plain language summaryDisability Justice is a set of 10 ideas written by disabled activists. It says disabled people should lead, everyone depends on each other, and access should be shared by everyone. We used these ideas to judge each post.

Disability Justice was developed by disabled queer and trans activists of color, including the performance project Sins Invalid, who felt the earlier disability rights movement centered mostly white, physically disabled people and focused on legal access. Disability Justice asks for more than laws. It asks for a world built around disabled people's leadership, care, and wholeness. Sins Invalid (2019) lays out ten principles:

  1. IntersectionalityDisability is shaped by race, gender, sexuality, class, and immigration status. No one experiences it in isolation.
  2. Leadership of those most impactedThe people living with a disability should lead the conversation about it.
  3. Anti-capitalist politicsWorth should not depend on how productive a body is, and access should not depend on wealth.
  4. Commitment to cross-movement organizingDisability Justice works alongside other justice movements.
  5. Recognizing wholenessDisabled people are whole people with full lives, histories, and interests.
  6. SustainabilityPace work and activism so bodies are not pushed past their limits.
  7. Commitment to cross-disability solidarityPhysical, sensory, intellectual, psychiatric, chronic, and invisible disabilities stand together.
  8. InterdependenceEveryone relies on others. Meeting each other's needs is normal, not a failure of independence.
  9. Collective accessAccess is everyone's responsibility, and it can be creative and shared.
  10. Collective liberationNo one is left behind. Liberation includes all bodies and minds.

The pattern is clear. The examples that challenge ableism are almost always made by disabled people speaking for themselves. The example that reinforces ableism is the one where no disabled person speaks at all. Leadership of those most impacted shows up in nearly every row, for or against.

Closing: What would less ableist social media look like?

Plain language summarySocial media can hurt or help disabled people. Better content lets disabled people tell their own stories, shows them as full people, and is accessible to everyone.

After looking at these examples, social media clearly has both positive and negative effects. It can reinforce stereotypes, and it can also challenge them by giving disabled people a platform to share their own experiences in their own way.

Less ableist content would let disabled people represent themselves instead of having others speak for them. It would show that disability is one part of someone's identity, and that disabled people have their own interests, personalities, relationships, goals, and experiences.

We already see this in the content on this page. The Spider-Man prosthetic leg turns assistive technology into personal expression. The autism essay challenges the idea that a diagnosis means something is wrong or needs to be changed. These examples show that disability representation does not have to focus on struggle or limitation. It can also show identity, independence, community, and creativity.

Less ableist social media should:

  • Give disabled people the chance to tell their own stories
  • Show disability in everyday life without turning ordinary activities into inspiration porn
  • Represent many disabilities and experiences, including invisible ones, instead of repeating the same stereotypes
  • Include accessibility features such as captions, image descriptions, audio descriptions, and transcripts
  • Focus on removing barriers and improving access instead of treating the disabled person as the problem

Connecting to Disability Justice

Each of these goals maps onto a Disability Justice principle. Letting disabled people tell their own stories is leadership of those most impacted. Showing disabled people as full people with hobbies and opinions is recognizing wholeness. Including captions and descriptions is collective access, where every creator shares responsibility for making content reachable. Defending people with disabilities we cannot see, as the parking video does, is cross-disability solidarity. And asking who can afford prosthetics or who gets their story told brings in anti-capitalist politics and intersectionality, since access online follows the same lines of money, race, and class as access offline.

Social media can either continue harmful assumptions or create a space where disabled people are respected, included, and heard. Disability content does not need to be about overcoming challenges. Disabled people should be able to share every part of their lives. The goal is not just to include disability in online content, but to represent disabled people accurately and give them room to define their own experiences.

Being respected is important. Being understood and included matters just as much.

Accessibility statement

Plain language summaryWe built this page so more people can use it. You can make the text bigger, add space between lines, use a screen reader, and read descriptions of every image and video.

A page about disability representation should be accessible itself. We treated access as part of the content, following the Disability Justice idea of collective access. Here is what we built in and why.

  • Readable typeface. Body text uses Atkinson Hyperlegible, a font designed by the Braille Institute so similar letters (like I, l, and 1) are easy to tell apart for readers with low vision.
  • Reading options. The bar at the top lets you enlarge text and widen line spacing. Your choice is remembered in your browser.
  • Image descriptions in layers. Every example has short alt text. Longer descriptions, audio descriptions, timed video descriptions, and transcripts are in expandable sections, so screen reader users and sighted readers can choose how much detail they want.
  • Plain language summaries. Each section opens with a short summary in simple words for readers with cognitive or learning disabilities, readers new to these ideas, and readers using translation tools.
  • Clear structure. Headings follow a logical order, sections are labeled for screen readers, and a "Skip to main content" link appears when you press Tab.
  • Color and contrast. Text colors meet the WCAG 2.2 AA contrast ratio of at least 4.5 to 1 in both light and dark mode. Labels such as "Challenges ableism" use words, not color alone.
  • Keyboard access. Every link, button, and expandable section works with a keyboard and shows a visible focus outline.
  • Reduced motion. The page has no autoplaying media or flashing content, and smooth scrolling turns off if your device asks for reduced motion.
  • Players load only when you choose. Embedded TikTok and Instagram players stay off until you press "Load the player," so video and sound never start on their own. Every embedded post is also fully described on this page, so you can follow each example without loading the player at all.
  • Captions in the original media. We note where the original posts have captions and where they fall short, such as the cut-off caption in case study 7.

Many of these ideas come from the author of our first case study, whose accessibility plan for their essay included screen-reader compatibility, alt text, adjustable font sizes, text-to-speech, high-contrast text, a plain-language summary, and a distraction-free reading option.
